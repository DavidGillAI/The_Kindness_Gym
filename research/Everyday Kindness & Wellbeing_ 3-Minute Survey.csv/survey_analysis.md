# Survey Analysis: Everyday Kindness & Wellbeing

## Methodology

An anonymous Google Forms survey was distributed
through the researcher's extended personal and social
network to explore everyday kindness habits, attitudes
towards The Kindness Gym and willingness to pay.

Final Round 1 dataset: 90 responses.

The survey contained 10 quantitative findings and
one optional written-feedback question. Thirty-four
respondents provided nonblank written feedback.

Participation was voluntary and respondents were
self-selected. This convenience sample is not
representative of the general population.

The findings measure reported behaviour, attitudes
and stated intentions. They do not establish actual
product adoption, willingness to purchase or
behavioural effectiveness.

**Result:** 35 of 90 respondents (38.9%) expressed

**Limitation:** These responses measure stated

Question: Which features, if any, would interest you?

| Feature | Respondents | Percentage |
|---|---:|---:|
| Progress tracking and habit reminders | 43 | 47.8% |
| Exercises for self-kindness | 39 | 43.3% |
| Personalised exercises based on everyday situations | 36 | 40.0% |
| Short research-based insights | 30 | 33.3% |
| A structured 21-day kindness course | 24 | 26.7% |
| Exercises for kindness towards others | 23 | 25.6% |
| None of these | 10 | 11.1% |

Two respondents provided additional comments through the Other option.

**Result:** Progress tracking and habit reminders attracted
the most interest, followed by self-kindness exercises
and personalised exercises.

The proposed 21-day course was selected by 24 of 90
respondents (26.7%).

**Interpretation:** Respondents expressed more interest
in practical exercises and habit support than in the
structured course itself.

This suggests testing whether the course should be the
main selling point or whether personalised exercises
and habit support should take greater prominence.

**Limitation:** Respondents could select multiple features,
so percentages do not add up to 100%. Stated feature
preferences do not establish actual usage or willingness
to pay.

## Finding 6: Concerns About an AI-Powered Kindness App

Question: What concerns, if any, would you have about using an AI-powered kindness app?

| Concern | Respondents | Percentage |
|---|---:|---:|
| Privacy and personal data | 54 | 60.0% |
| Inaccurate or inappropriate advice | 51 | 56.7% |
| Cost | 35 | 38.9% |
| Becoming too dependent on AI | 31 | 34.4% |
| Lack of human interaction | 30 | 33.3% |
| No concerns | 7 | 7.8% |

Seven additional responses raised other issues, including environmental impact, monetisation, effectiveness and time commitment.

**Result:** Privacy and personal data were the most frequently selected concern, followed closely by inaccurate or inappropriate advice.

**Interpretation:** Privacy and AI reliability should be central requirements for the pilot.

These findings reinforce the importance of the problems identified during our POC evaluation, particularly unreliable research selection and unsupported reassurance.

Environmental impact also emerged as an additional concern worth acknowledging.

**Limitation:** Respondents could select multiple concerns, so percentages do not add up to 100%. These results measure perceived risks, not the actual frequency of harmful outcomes.

|---|---:|---:|

Additional free-text responses included therapy, counselling, physical activity and other personal-development practices.

**Result:** Meditation or mindfulness was the most frequently selected approach, followed by self-help books or podcasts and journaling or reflection.

**Interpretation:** Many respondents have experience with personal-development practices. However, fewer have tried wellbeing apps or structured courses.

**Limitation:** Respondents could select multiple approaches, so percentages do not add up to 100%. Prior experience does not establish demand for The Kindness Gym.

**Data-quality note:** A small number of respondents selected "None of these" alongside other activities. Their responses have been retained as submitted.

## Finding 11: Qualitative Feedback

Of the 90 respondents, 34 provided nonblank written feedback. These responses were reviewed for recurring themes and suggestions.

### Emerging Themes

**1. AI scepticism and preference for human interaction**

Some respondents expressed distrust of AI guidance, describing it as potentially formulaic or insincere. Others preferred speaking with a person or interacting face to face.

**2. Translating kindness into real-world action**

One detailed response emphasised that the app should encourage meaningful kindness outside the phone rather than simply increase screen time. Suggestions included community connections and charity partnerships.

**3. Boundaries and accountability**

Respondents raised the importance of preventing kindness from becoming self-sacrifice. Suggestions included an accountability tracker and meaningful weekly goals.

**4. Different views on habit tracking**

Some respondents requested habit tracking, while another questioned its usefulness, arguing that tracking can become an additional habit to maintain.

**5. Alternative features and formats**

Suggestions included shared kindness challenges, an offline format, activities for children and exercises focused on particular themes such as patience.

**6. Barriers to engagement**

Respondents mentioned lack of personal need, inconsistent app usage and concern that the programme might require too much effort.

**7. Course length and accessibility**

One respondent felt that 21 days was too long, while
another indicated that a programme requiring too much
effort would discourage participation.

Others expressed interest in an easy-to-use and engaging
experience.

The pilot should test whether users find the proposed
course length manageable rather than assuming that
21 days is the ideal format.

**8. Alternative audiences and partnerships**

One respondent suggested making kindness practice
part of a workplace routine through business partnerships.

Other suggestions included adapting the concept for
children, providing opportunities for human connection,
and broadening the subject matter beyond kindness.

These are potential future directions rather than
requirements for the initial pilot.

### Interpretation

The feedback suggests that the pilot should test whether the app encourages useful real-world behaviour, provides credible guidance and remains simple to use.

The differing views on habit tracking mean that its value should be tested rather than assumed.

### Limitations

These themes come from 34 optional written responses
within the frozen 90-response dataset.

Respondents were recruited through an extended personal
and social network. Participation was voluntary and
self-selected, so the findings are exploratory and
cannot be generalised to the wider population.

Individual suggestions should not automatically become
product requirements. They should inform future user
testing and product-development decisions.
